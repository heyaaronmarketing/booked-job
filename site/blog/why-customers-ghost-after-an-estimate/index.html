Why Customers Ghost After an Estimate

AP By Aaron Phillips · Booked Job · Updated June 2026
Short answer: You quoted it, they said "let me think about it," and then nothing. It's usually not the price — it's that someone else got there first, the quote was hard to say yes to, or nobody followed up. All three are fixable this week.

The quote was hard to say yes to

One number and no next step invites delay.

A bare total makes a homeowner do the work of deciding. Give them scope in plain language, what's included and excluded, two options where you can (good / better), and an explicit next step with a date: "Reply yes and I'll hold Thursday the 12th." Saying yes should take one sentence.

They never trusted you enough to spend that much

Proof closes the gap price can't.

Big-ticket work needs proof attached to it: recent reviews, photos of the same job, license and insurance. 91% of homeowners won't even consider a shop under 4 stars (BrightLocal 2026) — that filtering doesn't stop once they've met you. Put the proof in the estimate, not just on the website.

Nobody followed up

Two touches recover jobs you already earned.

Most shops quote and wait. Send a short message at 48 hours ("any questions on the scope?") and one at day 10 ("holding a slot next week if you still want it"). No pressure, no discount — just presence. This is free money: you already paid to get the lead and did the site visit.

And check that you didn't miss the callback

The average shop misses 14% of its calls.

Sometimes they didn't ghost you — they called back and got voicemail. The average shop misses 14% of calls (CallRail 2026). Before you blame the price, look at the call log: missed calls cost invoices.

Frequently asked questions

Why do customers ghost after getting an estimate?

Usually not price. Most often someone responded faster (78% hire the first responder — Lead Connect 2026), the quote had no clear next step, there wasn't enough proof to trust a big spend, or nobody followed up. Missed callbacks account for more than owners think — the average shop misses 14% of calls (CallRail 2026).

How many times should I follow up on a quote?

Twice is the sweet spot: a short check-in at about 48 hours and one at around day 10 offering a specific slot. No discounting, no pressure — just a clear, easy yes.

Should I lower my price if they go quiet?

No. Discounting to chase silence trains customers to wait you out and eats the margin on the job. Fix speed, clarity, and proof first — those recover more quotes than a price cut.
